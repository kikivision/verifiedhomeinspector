This is the body copy that was briefly live on `/insurance-inspections/` and
was cut on 2026-09-11. It was removed for two reasons: it buried the county
picker, which is the point of the page, and it put a page of specific claims
in front of readers that nobody at Sunstate had independently checked.

It is kept here so nothing is lost and so any part of it can be verified
before it goes back. **None of it is on the live site.**

Sourcing is not uniform. Most of it came from primary sources. One section did
not, and that section is the one to be most careful about.

## Not sourced from a primary source — treat as unverified

The "If the 4-point turns something up" section listed Federal Pacific and
Zinsco panels, aluminum branch wiring, polybutylene supply plumbing, a roof
with little remaining life, and active leaks as findings that stop a policy.

That list came from **insurance-industry and inspection-company blog posts**,
not from a statute, a regulator, or a carrier's published underwriting guide.
It is widely repeated and is probably broadly right, but "widely repeated" is
not verification, and underwriting rules are set per carrier and change. On a
site whose entire promise is verified accuracy, this is the wrong kind of
claim to publish on someone else's authority.

If it is ever wanted back, verify against published carrier underwriting
guidelines or the Citizens Property Insurance underwriting manual, and say
plainly that it varies by carrier.

## Unsourced procedural description

"How it usually goes" and "What the inspector needs to reach" describe the
ordinary sequence of a carrier change and what an inspector needs access to.
Nothing in them is controversial, but nothing in them was checked against a
source either. They are reasonable descriptions, not verified facts.

## The removed copy, verbatim

### How it usually goes

Your agent shops carriers and comes back with what the quoting carrier wants.
You book the inspections and you pay for them. The inspector sends you the
report, and you forward it to your agent, who submits it with the application.
The carrier reads the reports before it binds the policy rather than after, so
nothing moves until they are in hand.

### Check that the inspector can sign the wind mitigation form

The wind mitigation report is not a document the inspector writes. It is a
state form, OIR-B1-1802, issued by the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation,
and the revision in use took effect on April 1, 2026. Not every licensed home
inspector is allowed to sign it. Section 627.711(2)(a) of the Florida Statutes
permits a home inspector licensed under section 468.8314 to complete the form
only after at least three hours of hurricane mitigation training, and OIR tells
homeowners to confirm an inspector is authorized under that subsection before
hiring one. It is a short question to ask, and the alternative is paying for a
report your carrier will not accept.

Before you book anything, check whether you already have one. OIR treats a
completed form as valid for up to five years, provided nothing material about
the structure has changed and nothing on the form turns out to be inaccurate.
A wind mitigation from a few years ago may still be doing its job.

### Why the same report is worth more at one carrier than another

The discounts are required to exist. Section 627.0629 says a residential
property rate filing must include actuarially reasonable discounts, credits, or
other rate differentials for verified features — roof shape, roof deck
attachment, roof-to-wall strength, opening protection, and the rest. What the
statute does not do is set how large they are. That is decided in each
carrier's own rate filing, which is why an identical form can be worth
noticeably more at one company than at another, and why it is worth asking your
agent what a particular carrier credits before you choose on headline premium
alone.

### What the inspector needs to reach

Plan on access to the electrical panel, the water heater, the air handler, and
the attic hatch, and clear a path around all four sides of the house. If you
have permit records or receipts for a roof replacement or a re-pipe, find them
before the visit. The age of the roof covering, and the permit date behind it,
are what several wind mitigation credits are scored against.

### If the 4-point turns something up

A few findings stop a policy rather than simply lowering a score. The usual
ones are a Federal Pacific or Zinsco electrical panel, aluminum branch wiring,
polybutylene supply plumbing, a roof with little remaining life, and any active
leak. None of those are statutory the way the roof-age rule is — they are
underwriting decisions and they vary by carrier — but they are common enough to
be worth knowing before a renewal deadline rather than a week after it. A
carrier will generally want the repair done and the report re-issued before it
binds.
